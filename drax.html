The Drax question.

Britain's biggest power station burns wood shipped three thousand miles from American forests — and officially, it is the country's biggest source of renewable power. Whether that sentence makes sense is one of the most genuinely contested questions in British energy. Both cases, made properly.

The journey of a pellet

Drax was built in the 1970s to burn coal from the pit next door. Today it burns compressed wood pellets — millions of tonnes a year, most of it crossing the Atlantic from the forests of the American South. Follow the journey, then we'll argue about it properly.

One tonne of wood, two worldviews — flip between them

Here is the genuinely strange thing: both sides of the biomass argument agree on the physics. Burning wood releases carbon — slightly more per unit of electricity than coal, at the chimney. The entire fight is about how you account for it. Press each worldview and read both fairly.

Where the honest middle sits
The serious scientific position is uncomfortable for both camps: the accounting can work, but only if the forests are genuinely regrowing, the timescales are honest, and the wood would not have stored more carbon left alone — conditions that are hard to verify across an ocean and that vary pellet by pellet. "Renewable" is doing a lot of work in the phrase "renewable power station," and how much work it can bear is a judgement, not a fact. This series can show you the argument; it cannot settle it. Nobody can yet.
Why the grid keeps it around anyway
Set the carbon argument aside for one paragraph and Drax is something rare: a huge, controllable power station in exactly the right place — the kind of dispatchable capacity the tight winter evenings of Post 26 are counted in. The system question and the climate question pull in different directions, which is precisely why the answer became a compromise.

The 2027 deal — and the bet behind it

The old deal
Until 2027
Drax converted from coal on generous renewable subsidies — running near-baseload, burning pellets around the clock, paid for every unit. It kept the lights on through the coal phase-out and became one of the most subsidised power stations in the country. Both of those things are true at once.
The new deal
2027 onwards
From 2027 the support roughly halves and the role flips: Drax becomes a low-load backstop — running far less, mainly when the system is tight, with stricter sustainability rules on what it burns. Less wood, less subsidy, less running — kept alive specifically for the still, dark evenings this series keeps returning to.
The bet
BECCS
Drax's endgame is bolting carbon capture onto biomass — BECCS — so that growing the wood absorbs carbon and burning it captures carbon: negative emissions, on paper. If it works at scale it becomes climate infrastructure; if it does not, the accounting argument above gets louder. It is one of the biggest engineering-meets-accounting bets in British energy, and it is still just that — a bet.

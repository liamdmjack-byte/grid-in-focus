Where does the wind go when nobody needs it?

Some nights Britain generates more clean power than it can use. Where does it go? Mostly nowhere — and fixing that is the next great build-out. The catch: batteries last hours, and Britain's wind droughts last weeks.

The phone battery problem
Imagine your phone battery lasted four hours, but you sometimes needed it to last three weeks. That is Britain's storage problem. Batteries are superb at the daily rhythm. The dark, still winter fortnight — the dunkelflaute — is a different problem entirely, and nothing built so far solves it.
Figure 02

What Britain has, what it is building

Every dot is half a gigawatt — filled dots exist, hollow ones are the build-out. The battery rows fill fast. The quieter story is the revival of another era's technology: pumped hydro schemes first sketched in the 1970s are finally being built, because they store ten times longer than lithium.

The mountain is back
Dinorwig — the Electric Mountain of the World Cup Special — was built in the 1970s to soak up overnight nuclear power. It is now the template for the renewables age: Coire Glas in the Highlands would be Britain's first new large pumped hydro in 40 years, storing enough to power 3 million homes for a day. The government's new cap-and-floor scheme exists specifically to get projects like it financed.

Hours versus weeks — the duration gap

Each technology is drawn at the duration it can sustain full output. The gap on the right side of this chart is the hardest unsolved problem in the entire energy transition — and the reason gas plants are staying on standby long after they stop running routinely.

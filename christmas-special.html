The reason it's the only day like this
The lunchtime peak isn't really about ovens. It's about absence — the shops, the offices, the factories and the schools that normally dominate daytime demand are all shut, all at once, the same day. What's left showing on the grid is mostly just homes. For one day, the national demand curve is the shape of family life, with the industrial hum that usually drowns it out switched off. You can see Christmas in the data.

The pickup that used to stop the nation

For decades, the control room watched the Christmas TV schedule as closely as the weather — because when the whole country watched the same thing at the same time, the moment it ended was visible from space. Those moments are fading, and that fading is its own quiet story.

1996
The legendary one
The Only Fools and Horses Christmas special — the one where Del Boy and Rodney finally became millionaires — drew over 20 million people to the same screens at the same time. When it ended, the grid jumped about 1,340 MW: a million-plus kettles and a nation putting the oven on for leftovers, all in the same few minutes. It is still the Christmas pickup everyone in the industry quotes.
The King's broadcast
The reliable one
At 3pm the afternoon message to the nation still produces a gentle, dependable bump — one of the last moments of the year when a large share of the country chooses to watch the same thing live. The control room still pencils it in. It is less a spike than a nod: the shape of a shared national habit that has mostly, quietly, gone.
Now
The streaming-era fade
The giant synchronised pickups are softening year on year — not because we watch less, but because we no longer watch together. On-demand means everyone's big moment lands at a slightly different minute, and a thousand small pickups smear into a gentle slope. The grid is quietly recording the end of appointment television. The kettle, at least, is forever.

The greenest day of the year

There's a happy accident hidden in all this. The lowest-demand day, falling in the windy heart of winter, is often the day the grid runs cleanest — and that has been true more and more each year.

A Christmas the grid would wish for
On a mild, blustery 25th, demand sits low while the wind turbines of Post 21 run hard — and the grid can tip toward running almost entirely on clean power for the day. Recent Christmases have set records for the lowest carbon intensity ever recorded, the festive feast cooked on something close to pure wind. It's a small, lovely thing: the one day we're most at home together tends to be the one day the grid is at its very best. Whatever you're cooking it on this year — the engineers in the control room of Post 22 are working through the day so you don't have to think about any of it. That, in the end, is the whole series in a sentence.

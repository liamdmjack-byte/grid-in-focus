The map of everything.

For thirty-five years, nobody decided where Britain's energy system should go — projects applied, queues formed, and the grid chased whatever showed up. That era is ending. The first national energy map since the CEGB is being drawn right now, and this year's decisions set its direction.

The plan of plans — tap each layer

For thirty-five years, Britain's energy map was drawn by whoever applied first — the queue chaos of Post 1 was the result. Now a stack of official plans is being built, each feeding the next. Tap each layer of the stack to see what it decides.

What's actually being decided
The deep shift is philosophical. Since 1990 the market proposed and the grid reacted; the result was a 700-gigawatt queue and pylons chasing projects. The new plans reverse the arrow: decide the shape of the system first, then invite the market to fill it in. It is the most CEGB-like thing Britain has done since the CEGB — done, this time, with markets inside it.

Why the slip is (honestly) fine
The delay happened because the cost data underneath the modelling — what wind, solar and storage actually cost to build, post-AR7 — moved enough that NESO and government chose to re-run the whole exercise rather than publish a map drawn with stale numbers. Annoying, defensible, and exactly the trade this series would argue for: a map people trust in 2027 beats a map people litigate in 2026. The pathways decision this summer keeps the direction set in the meantime.

What it means for actual places

Zones, not sites
The crucial nuance
The spatial plan will say "this kind of zone suits this much of this technology" — it will not name fields or pick projects. Developers still choose sites, communities still get consultations, the planning system of Post 9 still applies. It narrows the where; it does not abolish the argument.
Wires before requests
The practical payoff
If you know roughly where the generation is going, you can build the network there before the connection requests arrive — reversing the decades-old pattern where the grid is always the last to know. The network plan (CSNP) exists to do exactly that, and its transitional refresh landed this summer.
Your region gets a plan too
The local layer
The least-noticed layer may matter most: Regional Energy Strategic Plans, the first transitional versions launched in January, eventually one per region — heat, transport, local generation and the distribution networks of Post 6 planned together for the place you actually live. Strategic planning, all the way down to your postcode.

The 1928 competition
The lattice pylon was chosen in 1928 by an architectural competition — judged partly on elegance. The winning design was praised for its lightness against the sky. Nearly a century later it is so familiar it has become invisible: the largest piece of public design in Britain that nobody looks at.

Old faithful and the newcomer

In 2021 the first genuinely new pylon design in nearly a century went up in Somerset, carrying Hinkley Point C's power: the T-pylon. Shorter, tidier, no lattice — and surprisingly divisive.

The lattice (1928)
~50m · steel truss · the classic
Strong, cheap, repairable with a spanner, and climbs like scaffolding for maintenance. The open lattice lets wind through, which is why it survives storms that fell trees. Its weakness was never engineering — it was the planning system of Post 9: people simply do not want new ones on the skyline.
The T-pylon (2021)
~35m · single pole · the newcomer
A third shorter, with all six conductor bundles hung from two arms in a diamond arrangement. Designed to be calmer on the eye and faster to assemble. The honest scorecard: tidier in lowland landscapes, but it cannot carry as many circuits, costs more, and some engineers — and some locals — quietly prefer the original. Both will share the landscape for decades.

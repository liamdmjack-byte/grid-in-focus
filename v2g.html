The reframe
Post 7 worried about EVs as a load — millions of cars all charging at once. Flip it over: the same cars are the largest battery fleet the country will ever own, already bought, already parked on every street, waiting for a cable that works in both directions. The grid's problem and the grid's answer are the same machine.

Why 2026 is the year it got real

The certification
The boring breakthrough
Exporting to the grid needs official approval — the same G99 grid code as a solar farm. Most early bidirectional chargers failed it; of 14 UK type-test applications, 11 reportedly did not pass. Then Nissan secured the first G99 certification for cheap AC-based V2G, with a charger aimed at ordinary wallbox prices. Unsexy, decisive.
The car
£24k, V2G as standard
The Kia EV2 entered production in June with vehicle-to-grid built into every trim from around £24,000 — the first time the technology has shipped as standard in a mass-market car rather than a premium option. A growing list (Renault 5, VW ID family, Hyundai, Volvo) has the hardware waiting for tariffs and chargers to catch up.
The money and the mandate
Both directions at once
Octopus's Power Pack — the first proper V2G bundle — models savings around £620 a year, with some early users covering their whole electricity bill. Meanwhile a 2025 rule quietly requires V2G capability in new commercial charge points above 22kW. The carrot and the stick arrived in the same year.
The honest caveats
Three, plainly: bidirectional chargers are still expensive (the cheap ones are coming, not here); battery warranty terms vary and people reasonably worry about wear (the evidence so far suggests gentle V2G cycling is modest against normal driving, but "so far" is doing work in that sentence); and the whole thing only works if plugging in becomes as automatic as charging your phone. The technology is ready before the habits are.

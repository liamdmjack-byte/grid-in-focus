The shape of a storm — Éowyn's restoration curve

January 2025: Storm Éowyn set wind records and knocked out power to around a million customers across the island of Ireland and Scotland. The recovery has a characteristic shape — fast at first, then a long, stubborn tail. The tail is the story.

Drawing restoration curve
Network operator restoration reporting, Jan–Feb 2025
Why the tail is so long
The first 80% comes back fast because one fix — a tripped circuit re-closed, a main line repaired — restores thousands at once. The last few percent are the opposite: one fallen pole serving one farm at the end of one lane, times ten thousand lanes, in weather that hasn't finished. The maths of the tail is why "most customers restored within 48 hours" and "some homes off for over a week" describe the same storm.

The operation you never see
While you sleep through the storm, mutual aid is already moving: crews from unaffected network companies driving overnight toward the damage, an agreement older than privatisation. Helicopters fly the lines at first light because you cannot fix what you have not found. The people of Post 10 — the linesworkers, the engineers — are the whole story here, in a 4x4, driving toward the thing everyone else is driving away from.

Hardening the network — the three honest trade-offs

Bury the lines?
The obvious idea, costed
Underground cables don't blow over — but burying rural overhead lines costs many times the overhead equivalent, faults that do occur take far longer to find and fix, and Britain has hundreds of thousands of kilometres of the stuff. The honest answer is selective: bury the worst-exposed spans, not the map. Anyone promising "just underground it all" has not met the bill.
Cut the trees?
The unglamorous winner
Most storm damage is not wind snapping wires — it is trees falling on them. Vegetation management is the least exciting line in any network budget and one of the most effective: wider cleared corridors, resilience-trimming beyond the legal minimum, surveys by drone and laser. Arwen's inquiry pushed exactly this, and Éowyn was the first full test of the upgraded regime.
Let it heal itself?
The quiet revolution
The modern fix: automation. Smart switches that re-route power around damage in seconds — the feeder pillar of Post 28, given reflexes — mean many customers now experience a blink instead of a blackout, and crews arrive knowing which span failed. The network cannot stop the tree falling. Increasingly, it can stop you noticing.
What Arwen changed
Storm Arwen in 2021 left some homes off for more than a week and produced a reckoning: a full Ofgem review, compensation rules strengthened, welfare provision made standard — hot food vans, generators for the vulnerable, proper communication instead of silence. Éowyn was brutal, but the response around it — the army of crews, the welfare hubs, the faster information — was the post-Arwen system working as redesigned. Storms are the network's exam, and the syllabus changed in 2021.

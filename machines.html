The scale problem your brain has
A modern offshore turbine is taller than the Shard to the blade tip. The tip itself moves at over 180 mph — faster than a Formula 1 car — while the blade appears, from the shore, to be turning lazily. These are the largest rotating machines humans have ever built, and we built hundreds of them in a decade.

What January's record auction actually bought

Every year the government auctions contracts for new clean power. January's offshore round — AR7 — was the biggest in British (and European) history: 8.4 GW across eight projects, enough for roughly 12 million homes. Hover each block.

The two odd ones out
Tucked into the results: two floating wind farms of 100 MW each — Erebus in the Celtic Sea and Pentland off Scotland. Floating turbines sit on moored platforms instead of seabed foundations, opening water too deep to build in. They are small, expensive and experimental — and they are how Britain reaches the windiest seas it owns. Watch this space across the next decade.

The honest scoreboard

What went right
A record, against the trend
While auctions in Germany and the Netherlands struggled or failed outright, Britain's round was oversubscribed enough that the government doubled the budget mid-auction. Eight projects, £22bn of private investment, and the pipeline rebuilt after the empty round of 2023. Confidence is back.
The price truth
~£91 per MWh
The winning price was about 11% higher than the round before — turbines, steel and borrowing all cost more than they did. Offshore wind is still among the cheapest new power Britain can build, but the era of every auction being cheaper than the last has paused. Honest is better than triumphant.
The next mountain
AR8
Britain has roughly 31 GW of offshore wind built or contracted. The 2030 range needs 43–50 GW. That leaves around 12 GW to secure in the next auction — more than this record round delivered — while the grid of Posts 9 and 12 races to be ready to plug it all in.

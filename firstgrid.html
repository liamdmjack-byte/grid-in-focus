The uncomfortable comparison
Britain consented and built its entire first national grid — 4,000 miles, 26,000 pylons, untried technology, pick-and-shovel construction — faster than it now takes to get permission and build a single new line. The 1930s are not a manual. But they are proof that the constraint was never engineering.
Figure 03

What was actually different — honestly

The 1930s comparison gets used as a cheap gotcha. The honest version is more interesting: some advantages we should not want back, and some we could.

What we shouldn't want back
The price of 1930s speed
There was no planning system — the Town and Country Planning Act came in 1947. No environmental assessment, no meaningful consultation, dangerous working conditions and Depression-era wages. Routes went where the CEB decided. Communities along them got little say and less compensation. Some of the decade-long process in Post 9 exists for genuinely good reasons.
What was genuinely smarter
The transferable lessons
One standard pylon design — the 1928 competition winner from Post 19 — manufactured at scale instead of bespoke per route. Standing wayleave teams negotiating with landowners in parallel, not in sequence. A single empowered body with a clear mandate and its own financing. Modern echoes: ASTI's standardised approvals and the new spatial plan are quietly rediscovering all three.
What it bought
The payoff, then and now
Before the grid: 600-odd separate suppliers, incompatible voltages, electricity a town-by-town luxury. After: prices fell, access spread, and in 1939 the grid became a strategic asset that kept wartime factories running through the Blitz. The argument for today's build-out is the same shape — pay for wires once, unlock cheaper power for decades.
The moment it became one machine
The grid was designed as separate regional rings — engineers doubted they could run in sync. Then, on the night of 29 October 1937, control engineers quietly connected them all in an unauthorised experiment. It worked. By 1938 Britain ran, officially, as a single synchronised machine — the same machine, vastly enlarged, whose 50 Hz pulse this series covered in № 11.

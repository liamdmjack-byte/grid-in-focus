Why can it take 10 years to build a pylon?

Britain needs to build more electricity infrastructure in the next five years than it has in the last thirty. The technology exists. The money is approved. What's slowing it down is the process of getting permission to build anything at all.

~3 yrs
Pre-application
Before a single form is submitted, the developer must consult local communities, landowners, councils and statutory bodies. Multiple rounds. Environmental surveys. Route options. Objection responses. This phase alone can run for years.
Norwich to Tilbury: 20,000 pieces of feedback. 40,000-person petition. 10% of the route redesigned underground.
~2.5 yrs
Development Consent Order
The application goes to the Planning Inspectorate. A panel examines it for six months — public hearings, written evidence, cross-examination. Then a recommendation to the Secretary of State, who makes the final call. Legal challenges can add more time on top.
The DCO process was designed to be faster than traditional planning. For many projects it still takes 2 to 3 years.
~4 yrs
Construction
With consent granted, the developer must procure contractors, source equipment — transformers and high-voltage cable have lead times of years — then build across often challenging terrain. A 180km line means hundreds of towers, each needing foundations, steelwork and strung conductors.
High-voltage transformer manufacturing capacity is a global bottleneck. Lead times of 2 to 4 years are common.
~1 yr
Energisation
Before electricity can flow, the new infrastructure must be connected to the existing network, tested under load and formally accepted by NESO. Only then does the constraint it was built to relieve actually disappear — and the £1.8bn annual cost begin to fall.
Energisation sounds routine. For complex new substations, it can take the best part of a year.

The uncomfortable truth
Britain banned onshore wind in 2015 because of how pylons look. It reversed that decision nine years later. The same instinct is now adding years to the grid upgrades needed to carry the offshore wind that replaced it.

The projects that have to get built

Three projects that illustrate the different dimensions of the challenge — and what is at stake if they slip further.

Norwich to Tilbury
183 km overhead line · East Anglia to Essex
The longest new onshore transmission line in a generation. Needed to carry North Sea offshore wind into London and the South East. Consultation began 2022. DCO submitted 2025. Target operational: 2031. Over 40,000 people petitioned for alternatives — 10% of the route was redesigned underground.
DCO under examination
Eastern Green Link 2
436 km subsea HVDC cable · Scotland to England
A 2 GW subsea cable from Peterhead to Drax — the largest single electricity transmission project in UK history. Moves Scottish wind power south without fighting the planning battles of onshore pylons. Construction began 2024. Target: 2029.
Under construction
Hinkley to Seabank
57 km overhead line · Somerset to Bristol
Without this line, Hinkley Point C cannot export electricity at full capacity when it opens. DCO granted 2022 — one of the faster consenting processes in recent memory. Construction underway through the Somerset Levels. Target: 2028.
Under construction

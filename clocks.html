The strangest fact in this post
Daylight saving exists partly because of energy. The 1916 act was a wartime measure to save coal for the war effort — shift the clocks, use less artificial light. Britain's evening electricity peak has been rearranged twice a year, by law, for over a century — and the law is older than the National Grid itself.

The three years Britain didn't change the clocks

Between 1968 and 1971, Britain ran an experiment: stay on summer time all year. Most people have never heard of it. Tap through what happened — and why the argument it started has never actually ended.

What changed
1968–71 · British Standard Time
Lighter evenings all winter — sunset an hour later, the evening rush in daylight for more of the year. The cost: darker mornings, brutally so in the north. In Scotland, midwinter sunrise pushed toward 10am, and children walked to school in the dark for months. The experiment was real, national, and ran for three full winters.
What the data said
Genuinely mixed
Evening road casualties fell; morning casualties rose; the net effect was argued about for decades. The energy case was similarly murky — lighting demand shifted rather than vanished. When Parliament voted in 1970, the experiment ended not because the data was decisive but because the mornings were miserable and the data was not decisive enough to justify them.
Why it keeps coming back
The zombie debate
Every few years someone proposes "double summer time" and the argument reanimates — lighter evenings, tourism, energy, road safety versus Scottish mornings. The modern energy twist is new though: in a solar-heavy grid, evening light means evening generation. The clock question is quietly becoming a grid question again, a century after it started as one.
The Sunday morning ritual itself
A small operational gem: the grid notices the clock change within hours. Sunday's demand curve shifts visibly against the sun, and Monday's evening peak arrives steeper — millions of lighting and cooking loads landing closer together in the new darkness. The control room of Post 22 re-learns the nation's rhythm twice a year, every year, on schedule.

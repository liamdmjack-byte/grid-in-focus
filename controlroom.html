The room that runs Britain.

Right now, a team in a control centre in Berkshire is matching the country's electricity supply to its demand — to within fractions of a percent, every second. Most people have no idea the room exists. This is a day inside it.

A shift in the control room — tap through the day

The grid balances itself automatically in milliseconds — Post 11 covered that. But the shape of every half hour is decided by people: forecasters, engineers and traders in NESO's control centre. Here is one ordinary Tuesday, hour by hour.

The thing to understand
Electricity cannot queue. Every second, the amount generated must equal the amount used — there is no warehouse, no buffer, no later. The control room exists because somebody has to make that true, half hour after half hour, forever. It has worked so well for so long that almost nobody knows the room exists.

Who the room talks to

An "instruction" from the control room is a formal market message: turn up, turn down, stand by. They flow constantly — to power stations, wind farms, batteries, interconnectors and the big pumped hydro plants. Tap each one.

The operator itself just changed

What NESO is
Born October 2024
The National Energy System Operator — the body that runs the electricity system minute to minute and now plans the whole energy system long-term. It was carved out of National Grid and bought by the government: the first publicly owned system operator since the CEGB was broken up in 1990.
Why it changed
The referee problem
The old operator sat inside National Grid — a company that also owned the wires it was instructing power to flow through. Mostly fine, occasionally awkward, increasingly untenable as the operator's job grew into planning the entire transition. Independence fixed the optics and the incentives at once.
What it now carries
The to-do list of the decade
Beyond the control room: the Clean Power 2030 advice, the connections queue reform of Post 1, the strategic plans coming in Post 30, the Winter Outlook of Post 26, and — by the end of this year — the legal duty to be able to restart a dead grid (Post 33). Two years old, and arguably the most consequential organisation in British energy.

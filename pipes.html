What happens to the pipes?

Under your street there are probably two energy networks. One is being rebuilt for double the demand. The other — 280,000 kilometres of gas pipe, sixty years in the making — is facing the opposite question, and almost nobody is asking it out loud.

The other network under your street

This series has spent twenty-two posts on the electricity network. There is a second one down there — and it is a marvel: 280,000 km of pipes, from seven-bar steel trunk lines to the yellow plastic outside your kitchen, delivering more energy on a cold winter day than the electricity grid does. The question is what happens to it.

The uncomfortable symmetry
Britain is rebuilding one network for double the demand while quietly wondering what to do with another built for demand that is going away. Both run under the same streets. Both are paid for by the same households. Only one of them has a plan.
Figure 03

The hydrogen verdict — honestly

For years the hopeful answer was "the pipes will carry hydrogen instead." The evidence has now largely come in, and it splits cleanly by use.

Heating homes — no
The trials that did not happen
Two "hydrogen village" trials were planned to test heating real streets with hydrogen. Whitby, near Ellesmere Port, was cancelled after residents objected. Redcar followed when the hydrogen supply could not be secured. The independent evidence reviews kept reaching the same conclusion: heat pumps deliver the same warmth with three to five times less energy. The dream of swapping the boiler and keeping everything else has, on the evidence, quietly ended.
Industry — yes
Where it genuinely works
Some processes need a burning gas or a chemical feedstock and cannot simply plug in — parts of chemicals, refining, glass, possibly high-temperature heat. Hydrogen produced from renewable power has a real job there, clustered around industrial sites like Teesside and Merseyside rather than piped to every postcode. Parts of the existing network may be repurposed to serve exactly these clusters.
Storage — maybe
The interesting one
Post 13's hardest problem was the multi-week wind lull. Hydrogen made on windy days and stored in salt caverns — then burned in power stations during the dark fortnight — is one of the few credible answers at that scale. Unproven economics, real physics. If the gas network has a long-term future, this is probably its shape: fewer pipes, bigger purpose.
The endgame question
Here is the question with no official answer: if gas demand falls far enough, who pays to safely decommission a 280,000 km network — and who pays the bills of the last households left on it, likely those least able to switch? Other countries are starting to plan "managed exit" street by street. Britain has not started. It is the biggest infrastructure question nobody is asking — which is exactly why it is in this series.

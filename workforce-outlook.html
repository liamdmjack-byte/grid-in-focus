Britain needs 400,000 energy workers. It doesn't have them.

The money for the Great Grid Upgrade is approved. The technology exists. But you cannot build a pylon without the people to build it — and right now, Britain doesn't have nearly enough of them. The bottleneck isn't pylons or planning. It's electricians.

The roles that need filling — and how long they take to train

More than half the 400,000 roles needed are equivalent to A-levels or below. This is not a shortage of PhDs. It is a shortage of electricians, line workers and civil engineers — people who take years to train and whose skills are being competed for globally. The global problem
Germany, Denmark, France, the US and Australia are all running the same upgrade programmes simultaneously. Every country is fishing in the same pool of experienced engineers.
The uncomfortable maths
A high-voltage engineer takes up to 7 years to train from scratch. Britain needs thousands of them by 2030. We should have started training them in 2023.

Why you can't just train faster
High-voltage engineering and substation work require supervised on-the-job experience that cannot be compressed. A course can teach theory in months. Competence — the kind trusted to work on live 400kV equipment — takes years of supervised practice. The only way to solve this by 2030 is to have started in 2022.

It is not just people — the equipment queue is just as long

The workforce shortage is matched by a global equipment shortage. High-voltage transformers, subsea cable and switchgear are all in short supply worldwide. Britain is not the only country placing orders.

The reframe
A smart meter does not save you money. It is a key. What saves money is what the key unlocks — tariffs that pay you to run the dishwasher at 2am, charge the car when wind is cheap, and get paid for cutting back during a Saving Session. No key, no door.
Figure 02

What it knows — and what it doesn't

The privacy worry deserves a straight answer, not a brush-off. Here is exactly what travels from the box on your wall, where it goes, and what it cannot see.

What it sends
Over a dedicated secure network
Your total electricity and gas usage, in half-hour blocks, to your supplier — via the DCC, a dedicated national network built for exactly this, separate from the internet. You control whether your supplier gets half-hourly, daily or monthly detail. That's the inventory: kilowatt-hours and timestamps.
What it cannot see
The myths, answered
It does not know which appliances you own, what you watched, or who is home — it sees one number for the whole house. It cannot be used to disconnect you remotely without the same legal process as before. And the old myth that "they'll switch you off at peak times" describes something no GB supplier can do to a standard credit meter.
The honest caveats
Two things the rollout got wrong, said plainly: early first-generation meters (SMETS1) sometimes went "dumb" when you switched supplier — a remote upgrade programme has now migrated most of them. And in some homes, especially in poor signal areas, the in-home display simply never worked well. Neither breaks the tariff logic — but both cost the rollout a decade of goodwill.

Why the grid is desperate for you to have one

The national reason is bigger than the personal one. Every flexibility scheme in this series — the World Cup Special's paid sessions, the demand shifting that avoids new pylons — runs on half-hourly data. A flexible grid without smart meters is a car without a speedometer.

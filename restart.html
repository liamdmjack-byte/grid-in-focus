Restart the grid yourself — five steps

Post 11 was about staying at 50Hz — the immune system. This is the resurrection: what happens if everything goes down. You cannot just switch a grid back on, because most power stations need grid power to start. Step through how you actually do it.

The bootstrapping problem, plainly
A big power station needs tens of megawatts just to switch itself on — pumps, fans, control systems. Normally it takes that from the grid. If the grid is dead, nothing can start because nothing is on. Black start is the answer to a riddle: how do you light the first match when every match needs a lit match? Answer: keep a few special matches that strike themselves — and pay them to wait, possibly forever.

The five-day clock — the new law, drawn

From the end of this month, restoration stops being a best-effort plan and becomes a legal standard: 60% of every region back within 24 hours, everything within five days. Here is the line the system must now be able to beat.

Why a law for something that has never happened?
Because the grid that never failed was the old grid — heavy spinning machines, predictable physics. The new one runs on electronics, weather and cables to other countries, and Europe got the reminder: in April 2025, Spain and Portugal lost their entire system in about five seconds and spent the better part of a day restoring it. Iberia recovered well — and the lesson was still sharp: you rehearse the resurrection before you need it, not after. Britain's deadline for being demonstrably ready is this month.

The new tricks in the playbook

Hydro is king
The classic self-starters
Opening a valve needs almost no electricity — which is why hydro plants are the royalty of black start. Scottish hydro and the pumped storage of the World Cup Special can wake themselves with batteries and falling water, then bring whole regions up behind them. Some of Britain's restoration contracts trace their lineage to plants built in the 1950s.
The world-first
Distributed ReStart
The genuinely new chapter: Britain pioneered restarting the grid bottom-up — wind farms, batteries and small generators on local networks forming live islands and growing them, rather than waiting for big stations to reach them. World-first trials proved it works; restoration services from distributed sources are now being procured for real. The grid that runs on renewables learning to resurrect on them too.
The hardest part isn't electrical
The five-day reality
A total shutdown is also no traffic lights, fading phone networks, water pumps on backup, payment systems down. The five-day standard exists because society's tolerance is measured in days, not weeks — and the drill includes the unglamorous parts: fuel for backup generators, radio links that work without the grid, and people who have rehearsed doing all of this in the dark.
